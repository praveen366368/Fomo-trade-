# Fees and Odds Sink the $1-Per-Coin Plan

The plan as written will lose money, and the team's own test agrees with the outside evidence. In a 45-minute live paper run on 19 Nov 2026, the 20-agent engine checked about 4,860 leads, bought 5 coins at $1 each, and **lost on all 5: $0.93 gone, about 19% per trade**. No coin rose more than 34% on screen ([run summary](../docs/paper-runs/2026-11-19-run1/summary.json)). The wider market explains why. In a live sample of 255 new Solana coins, **only 1 touched 10x within an hour**, most never traded again, and the coins that doubled gave almost all of it back within the hour. The early gains go to coin creators and their bundled wallets, who made **at least $579M against $31.5M for sniper bots** ([Bitquery](https://bitquery.io/investigations/pump-fun-snipers-bundles)). **About 90% of FOMO app wallets lost money after fees in 2026** ([Bitquery](https://bitquery.io/investigations/fomo-app-traders-lose-money)). At $1 a trade, costs alone take roughly 3% to 13% per round trip for a direct bot and about 34% with common trading-app settings, which is more than the average coin pays back. FOMO itself is real and well funded (about $94M raised, about 2.5M users), but its terms ban bots and scraping, spot trades are market orders only, and a $2 minimum order plus a minimum fee of up to $0.95 rule out $1 trades in the app. The "$160 into $100k" FOMO traders are company-told stories that cannot be checked, and they win by doing the opposite of this plan: few coins, later entry, bigger bets, slow staged selling. The engine itself is well built. The team should keep it in paper mode and fix the two flaws the run exposed. It should prove any edge on a replay of 10,000+ recorded entries before spending real money, and use $2–$5 stakes and a separate burner wallet if it ever goes live. It must also budget for India's 30% tax with no loss offset, which turns a break-even bot into a losing one.

## About 1 new coin in 250 hits 10x, and insiders get there first

Solana makes new meme coins at a huge rate. Bitquery counted **about 11.5 million new Solana tokens from 1 Nov 2025 to 7 Oct 2026, 78% of them from pump.fun**, roughly 34,000 a day ([Bitquery](https://bitquery.io/investigations/solana-launchpad-year)). Almost all of them die fast. **79% of pump.fun coins never traded again after launch day**, and only **1 in 58 "graduated"**: it filled pump.fun's starting price curve and moved to a real trading pool ([Bitquery](https://bitquery.io/investigations/solana-launchpad-year)). CoinGecko found that **only 4.55% of 18.67 million pump.fun coins were still trading after 90 days** ([KuCoin/CoinGecko](https://www.kucoin.com/news/flash/coingecko-study-only-4-55-of-pump-fun-tokens-survive-90-days)). In May–June 2026 the graduation rate fell as low as **0.198%** of 832,941 launches ([arXiv 2607.02823](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.02823v3)). On 9 Nov 2026, **just 312 of about 24.4 million pump.fun coins were worth more than $100,000** ([TokenPost](https://www.tokenpost.com/news/investing/54556)).

The team's researchers checked this live on the day of the paper run. They followed **255 Solana coins created between 19:12 and 19:21 UTC on 19 Nov 2026**. Each was "bought" about 6 minutes after it appeared and watched for an hour ([GeckoTerminal new pools](https://api.geckoterminal.com/api/v2/networks/solana/new_pools?page=1)). The typical coin went nowhere. The median ended at exactly 1.00x, and 66% had no trades at all in the final hour. About **10% fell more than 90%**. Only **6.3% touched 2x, 2.4% touched 5x, and a single coin (0.4%) touched 10x**. None reached 100x. The coins that doubled gave back a median **98.5% of their peak** before the hour ended. So "thousands of percent in minutes" does happen. But a 10x (+900%) is about a 1-in-250 event, and the window to sell lasts a few minutes. This was one weekday evening in a weak market (SOL about $74), so the rates will swing with the mood of the market. The direction will not.

Who gets those few big moves? Coins that graduate do it in a median **1.66 minutes** from launch ([arXiv 2607.02823](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.02823v3)). Right after graduating, **38% fall 80% within 10 minutes**, and the big dumps come at a median 2.2 minutes ([DEV Community](https://dev.to/launchtape/what-2193-pumpfun-graduations-did-in-their-first-10-minutes-and-what-it-means-for-your-bot-4f70)). Coin creators and their bundled wallets get their supply cheap or free, and they took the $579M. Regular buyers lose. Of **2.9 million Solana meme-coin buyers, 74% held less SOL two weeks later**, and the median loss was $13. **Spreading money across more coins did not help** ([Bitquery](https://bitquery.io/investigations/solana-memecoin-traders-lose-money)). Solidus Labs found that **98.6% of 7M+ pump.fun coins collapsed** to under $1,000 of liquidity, though pump.fun disputes the label ([Solidus Labs](https://www.soliduslabs.com/reports/solana-rug-pulls-pump-dumps-crypto-compliance)). The one cheerful number is CoinGecko's "73% of pump.fun wallets profitable" in April 2026. It counts only coins people actually sold, so it leaves out every bag still held at a loss ([CoinGecko](https://www.coingecko.com/research/publications/pump-fun-traders-are-making-a-comeback)). The plain lesson: putting $1 into many coins does not fix a game where the average coin loses. It only makes the loss smoother.

## FOMO is a big, real app, but it rules out this plan

### A funded company with strict rules

FOMO is run by **FOMO Labs Inc., a Delaware company** ([fomo Terms](https://fomo.family/terms)). It has raised **about $94M**. That includes a $17M Series A led by Benchmark in Nov 2025 ([fomo blog](https://fomo.family/blog/news/series-a)) and a $75M Series B led by Index Ventures in June 2026, at a reported **$550M valuation** ([The Block](https://www.theblock.co/news/deals/2026-06-22-crypto-trading-app-fomo-raises-75-million-at-550-million-valuation-in-index-ventures-led-series-b-report-405563)). By its own count it had **about 2.5 million users in Nov 2026**, though it does not say whether that means sign-ups or active traders ([fomo vs Moby](https://fomo.family/blog/compares/fomo-vs-moby)). Wallets are self-custody Privy wallets. The key is split into pieces, and the user can export it ([fomo security guide](https://fomo.family/blog/guide/fomo-security-wallet-architecture)). There is no confirmed hack. In 2026 someone claimed a $6M drain through the iOS app. A co-founder denied it, and no proof was ever shown ([Protos](https://protos.com/crypto-trading-platform-fomo-denies-hack-of-its-ios-app/)). A real danger does exist, though: a fake lookalike site, fomoo.family, drains the wallets of people who connect to it ([MalwareTips](https://malwaretips.com/blogs/fake-fomo-trading-platform-scam-how-fomoo-family-drains-connected-crypto-wallets/)).

Three rules block the plan. First, **the Terms ban bots, scripts, scrapers and sharing session tokens** "except through functionality we expressly authorize". FOMO can cut sessions or close accounts it suspects ([fomo Terms §4, §16](https://fomo.family/terms)), and it has no public API. Second, **spot trades are market orders only**. Spot has no take-profit, stop-loss or limit orders, and "copy trading" is just an alert you act on by hand ([fomo vs Moby](https://fomo.family/blog/compares/fomo-vs-moby)). Third, the money. The fee is **0.5% per side (0.45% with a referral code), with "a minimum of up to 0.95 USDC on smaller Solana trades"** ([fomo vs Axiom](https://fomo.family/blog/compares/fomo-vs-axiom)). The app's own code sets a **$2 minimum buy and sell** ([fomo web code](https://fomo.family/assets/manifest-90574f0d.js)). A $1 order will likely be refused, and a position that shrinks below $2 may not be sellable in the app. India is not on FOMO's banned-country list, and using a VPN to get around its limits is banned ([fomo Terms §1](https://fomo.family/terms)).

FOMO's own users show what its crowd achieves. Bitquery traced **829,539 Solana wallets that traded through FOMO from Jan to Oct 2026. About 10% were profitable after fees, and the median wallet lost $25.** FOMO's fees, about $40M, made up only about 10% of those losses. The rest came from the coins themselves ([Bitquery](https://bitquery.io/investigations/fomo-app-traders-lose-money)).

### What $1 really costs

Trading directly on-chain avoids FOMO's minimums, but $1 is still tiny next to fixed costs. pump.fun charges **1.25% per side** on its curve ([pump.fun fees](https://pump.fun/docs/fees)). Each open coin locks about **0.002 SOL (about $0.15) of account rent**, which comes back when the account is closed ([Raydium docs](https://docs.raydium.io/solana-fundamentals/rent-and-reclaimable-rent)). A typical 0.001 SOL "landing tip" alone costs about 7% of $1 per side ([Helius Sender](https://www.helius.dev/docs/sending-transactions/sender)). Common trading-terminal defaults add a 0.001 SOL priority fee plus a 0.001 SOL tip to every trade ([Axiompedia](https://axiompedia.com/guides/trading/best-axiom-trade-settings)), which costs more than the coin can pay back.

| How you trade $1 | Cost of one buy plus one sell | Coin must rise about this much to break even |
|---|---|---|
| FOMO app | Blocked by the $2 minimum; a $0.95 minimum fee would take 95% | — |
| Trading-terminal defaults (fixed fee and tip, ~1% app fee) | about 34% | +35% |
| Our paper engine (real Jupiter quotes, plus 3% landing drift each way) | about 9–13% on the coins it bought; more on thin coins | +10–15% |
| Lean direct bot, perfect landing | about 3.3% | +3.5% |

The lean figure is a floor, not a forecast. It assumes the price does not move while the trade lands. During research, a real Jupiter quote on a freshly graduated coin returned $0.933 for $0.994 put in, a 6% gap on one side alone ([Jupiter live quote](https://lite-api.jup.ag/ultra/v1/order?inputMint=So11111111111111111111111111111111111111112&outputMint=8MhvFChnHk55N286euYcE6pWNw8nRu1hLdJ11512pump&amount=13500000)). In the paper run, two coins were rejected because a plain buy-then-sell quote lost 12.4% and 14.6% ([filter audit](../docs/paper-runs/2026-11-19-run1/filter_audit.json)). Even at the lean 3.3%, the 255-coin sample still lost money with every exit rule (see the exit table below). The smallest stake that keeps fixed costs under 2% is about **$2 for a simple buy and sell, and about $5 for a sell ladder** ([STRATEGY.md](../docs/STRATEGY.md)). There is one small upside. At $1, sandwich bots barely bother you, because a 10% slippage limit risks only $0.10. Still keep the limit tight: those bots took **$29.7M** from wide-slippage meme trades in 11 months ([Bitquery](https://bitquery.io/investigations/solana-sandwich-attacks)).

## Our paper run: five buys, five losses, and two hidden flaws

The engine ran on live public data from **19:37 to 20:22 UTC on 19 Nov 2026**, in paper mode, with $1 stakes and a $250 paper balance ([summary.json](../docs/paper-runs/2026-11-19-run1/summary.json)). It took in about 4,860 leads (a coin can count more than once), and its watch list filled up to the 400-coin cap. The cheap checks did most of the cutting: LiquidityGuard passed only **6.4% of 21,718 checks**. **145 coins reached the deep checks.** OrganicFlow failed **119 of them (82%)** for weak signs of real buyers. RugGuard failed 20, HolderAnalyst 22 and HoneypotTester 2. In the end **only 5 coins, 3.4% of the deep-checked ones, were bought**. The free data stack held up well: 1,785 API calls, zero errors, and 14 rate-limit hits, 13 of them from GeckoTerminal.

| Coin | Held | Best on screen | Screen at sale | Got back for $1 | Why sold |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| JETSKI | 5.0 min | +4.4% | +2.1% | $0.89 | 5-min time stop |
| GTABANK | 5.0 min | never above entry | −13.3% | $0.90 | 5-min time stop |
| CATMARIO | 5.0 min | never above entry | −7.2% | $0.83 | 5-min time stop |
| OGTA | 5.1 min | never above entry | −11.5% | $0.83 | 5-min time stop |
| ONI | 9.2 min | +33.7% | +5.2% | $0.63 | end of run |
| **Total** | | | | **$4.09 back for $5.02 spent** | **−$0.93, about −19% per trade** |

Sources: [trades.log](../docs/paper-runs/2026-11-19-run1/trades.log) and [ledger.jsonl](../docs/paper-runs/2026-11-19-run1/ledger.jsonl). Network fees were only $0.04 for all 10 transactions, and each coin's $0.15 rent came back when it was sold.

Read this carefully, because five trades prove almost nothing about skill. No coin came near the 1.5x level where the trailing stop switches on, so four of five left at the 5-minute "it never moved" stop. Suppose each coin had the 6.3% chance of touching 2x seen in the live sample. Then five buys would see no 2x at all about 72% of the time. The run does not show the filters are bad. It shows how costs behave. The engine's 3% landing drift each way is a deliberate worst-case guess, harsher than the research's lean-bot estimate, and it explains about 5 of the 19 points lost. Take it out and all five trades still lose, by about 14% each on average, because prices drifted down and the real sell quotes came in below the screen price.

**The screen price is not the price you can sell at.** The engine decides exits from Dexscreener's price, which can be up to 30 seconds old, but it sells at Jupiter's real quote ([orchestrator.cpp](../engine/src/app/orchestrator.cpp)). Across the five sales, the real quote ranged from **37.5% below to 7% above** the screen price, even before the modelled drift. ONI showed +5% on screen at the end but sold for $0.63. Nobody can tell whether its +34% peak was ever sellable, because the engine never asked for a quote at that moment. The stop-loss and the trailing stop watch the screen price, so in live trading they would fire on prices nobody will pay. That is the first flaw: before any exit, check a real sell quote.

The second flaw is a leaky "hard" reject. The strategy promises that a hard-rejected coin is "never reconsidered" ([STRATEGY.md](../docs/STRATEGY.md)). Yet **OGTA was hard-rejected by HoneypotTester because a buy-and-sell quote lost 12.4%, and was later bought at a perfect score of 100**. It then lost $0.18, the worst of the four time-stopped trades ([filter_audit.json](../docs/paper-runs/2026-11-19-run1/filter_audit.json); [trades.log](../docs/paper-runs/2026-11-19-run1/trades.log)). The likely cause is the full watch list. When it hits 400 coins, the engine throws out rejected coins first. When OGTA showed up again as a boosted coin, it came back with a clean record ([orchestrator.cpp](../engine/src/app/orchestrator.cpp)). The fix is to keep hard rejects in a permanent list outside the watch list. The Auditor already keeps one.

The filter audit follows rejected coins afterwards, and it brought early good news. **GTA, rejected by RugGuard because its creator had rugged coins before, fell 99.4% during the run.** Research had already flagged the same coin: 10 earlier launches by its creator and an organic score of zero ([Jupiter token data](https://lite-api.jup.ag/tokens/v2/search?query=8MhvFChnHk55N286euYcE6pWNw8nRu1hLdJ11512pump)). All six coins cut by the deep checks fell afterwards, and none reached 2x. LiquidityGuard's "older than 24 hours" rule did skip the one rejected coin that later doubled (Mandy, +157%). It was one of 22 old coins that rule cut ([filter_audit.json](../docs/paper-runs/2026-11-19-run1/filter_audit.json)). Three agents never ran. The copy-trade wallet list is empty, so SmartMoney and TraderScout had nothing to do, and NarrativeAI (Jev) had no key. One more detail: the configured time stop leaves coins that stay below 1.15x after 5 minutes, but the simulated rule the team picked used 1.3x ([fomo.conf](../config/fomo.conf)).

Finally, pace. Five buys in 45 minutes works out to about 160 a day. The research model says telling a 20-cent-per-trade edge from zero takes tens of thousands of trades; the model puts it near 90,000 ([ROADMAP.md](../docs/ROADMAP.md)). Even 10,000 trades would take about two months of nonstop live paper trading. Proof has to come from replaying recorded data many times over, not from waiting for live trades.

## The "$160 to $100k" FOMO traders win by doing the opposite

Nobody in the evidence matches "$1–$100 to lakhs in 3–4 months" with checked on-chain proof. The closest is **Spyzer, who FOMO says "turned $160 into nearly $100k in a single month"**, but no wallet or trade record was published ([fomo blog](https://fomo.family/blog/news/trenches-are-dead)). **0xGolden** reportedly went from $1,000 to $3,000 in two months, lost it, then reached five figures in about three months. But the same quotes appear word for word in FOMO's profile of another trader, Printgod, so at least one of the two stories is credited to the wrong person ([fomo: 0xGolden](https://fomo.family/blog/trading-strategies/golden-crypto-trading-from-zero-barbell-strategy); [fomo: Printgod](https://fomo.family/blog/trading-strategies/printgod-crypto-trading-guide-new-traders)). The on-chain media stories are single lucky bets, often with insider claims: $160 to $6.14M on Chill Guy in 12 days ([Benzinga](https://www.benzinga.com/24/11/42238227/trader-turns-160-into-6m-in-just-12-days-with-chill-guy-meme-coin-but-insider-trader-concerns-arise)), or $321 to $2.18M on one coin, counted before any selling ([CryptoPotato](https://cryptopotato.com/this-trader-turned-321-into-2-18m-and-reminded-crypto-how-wild-it-still-is/)). The base rate is harsh. By Jan 2025 only **0.4% of 13.4 million pump.fun wallets had made $10,000 or more** ([Decrypt](https://decrypt.co/300403/pump-fun-traders-millionaires)). Leaderboards show the people who survived, not the much larger crowd who tried the same thing and lost.

What these traders say they do is the opposite of the $1 plan. Washed says "Avoid new pairs" and buys coins at **$400K–$3M market cap** ([fomo: Washed](https://fomo.family/blog/memecoins/memecoin-trading-strategies-lessons-from-six-figure-trader)). Logjam takes his starting money out after a 2x and lets the rest ride ([fomo: Logjam](https://fomo.family/blog/memecoins/logjam-meme-coin-penguin-trade)). Spyzer holds swing trades for 3–7 days. He wins only about 38% of them and makes up for it with bigger wins ([fomo: Spyzer](https://fomo.family/blog/memecoins/spyzer-swing-trading-memecoin-holding-strategy)). llonchain warns that "$1,000 spread across 20 positions… cannot move your life" ([fomo: llonchain](https://fomo.family/blog/trading-strategies/llonchain-aggressive-crypto-concentrated-betting-strategy)). And 4pfCoyote lost $250K, partly by chasing "19 people you follow bought" alerts ([fomo: 4pfCoyote](https://fomo.family/blog/trading-strategies/coyote-250k-loss-solana-chain-reading-lessons)). Their pattern is few coins, later entries, bigger bets, held for hours or days and sold in steps. That is a different strategy from many tiny bets sold within minutes.

Copying them does not work well either. Smart-money wallets averaged about 14% returns, while their copiers kept about 3% after costs ([arXiv 2601.08641](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.08641v2)). Following the earliest group-buy signal lost money in **all 32 setups tested** ([arXiv 2610.39458](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39458)), and copies placed two minutes late lost **41% per coin** ([TokenPost](https://www.tokenpost.com/news/investing/35409)). On FOMO, every follower gets the same alert in the same second, so the followers become the buyers the leader sells to. To study these traders anyway, do it by hand. FOMO's Positions leaderboard can be filtered by amount invested ([fomo release notes](https://fomo.family/release-notes)). Its trades leaderboard groups trades by size, starting at $100–$1k; there is no group for trades under $100 ([fomo leaderboard code](https://fomo.family/assets/leaderboard-v2-jB38GK1g.js)). FOMO's own advice is to favour 30-day consistency, many trades, a 40–60% win rate and trade sizes like yours ([fomo Answers](https://fomo.family/answers/how-to-find-profitable-crypto-traders)). Pulling that data with a script breaks the Terms. The engine already gives a tracked wallet's buy only a +15 score bonus and still makes the coin pass every filter, and that is the right weight.

## Keep the engine, change the plan

The 20-agent design follows the research. Each agent has one job, and cheap checks run before costly ones. An OCaml risk gate blocks every buy if it gets no answer. The AI can only nudge a score ([ARCHITECTURE.md](../docs/ARCHITECTURE.md)). The problems are in the plan's targets, not in the engine's shape.

### Free data works for paper trading, not for the first minutes

In the paper run, Dexscreener answered in 0.21 s on average and RugCheck in 2.5 s. Jupiter took 4.6 s, mostly waiting on its small free request limit. GeckoTerminal took 6.3 s and rate-limited 20% of calls ([summary.json](../docs/paper-runs/2026-11-19-run1/summary.json)). That is fine for coins that are at least 3 minutes old, which the engine targets on purpose. It is far too slow for the first two minutes, when graduations happen. Two changes are due before going live. First, Jupiter's free lite-api is being slowly throttled toward retirement. The engine should move to api.jup.ag with a free key (1 request per second, or $25/month for 10) and to the March 2026 Swap API v2 `/order` and `/execute` calls ([Jupiter changelog Nov 2025](https://developers.jup.ag/changelog/2025-11); [Jupiter changelog Mar 2026](https://developers.jup.ag/changelog/2026-03); [Jupiter rate limits](https://developers.jup.ag/docs/portal/rate-limits)). Second, faster data costs money, so time the purchase. pump.fun's own API blocked research requests behind Cloudflare ([live test](https://frontend-api-v3.pump.fun/coins/latest)). PumpPortal's new-coin websocket is free ([Solana Compass](https://solanacompass.com/projects/pumpportal)), and Helius Developer or a Chainstack gRPC stream each cost $49/month ([Helius plans](https://helius.dev/docs/billing/plans); [Chainstack](https://chainstack.com/yellowstone-grpc-more-streams-same-price/)). Buy faster data only after a replay shows that earlier entries raise the average result. When the average is near zero, every monthly fee is a straight loss.

### Filters: hard traps first, then the first five minutes

Some checks are yes-or-no traps where one miss costs 100%. These include an active mint or freeze authority, and Token-2022 features such as a permanent delegate (lets the creator move or burn your coins), a transfer hook (can make sells fail) or a non-transferable flag ([DEV Community](https://dev.to/mrwizardlyloaf/token-2022-traps-that-drain-ai-trading-agents-and-how-to-screen-them-33bo)). The engine already rejects all of these. The best-studied warning signs come from the **first five minutes of trading**. Machine-learning models built on early buy and sell counts, unique buyers and price change caught rugs with F1 scores of about 0.72–0.79 on the venue they were trained on, but only about 0.38 on a different venue ([arXiv 2608.20271](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20271v1)). Holder counts hide a lot. Once linked wallets are merged, the true top-10 share of risky coins averaged **24% higher** than it looked ([MemeTrans summary](https://lacuna.tiptreesystems.com/work/memetrans-a-dataset-for-detecting-high-risk-memecoin-launches-on-solana/wrk_9bae5b5f543b1331ffd891ca59a05ee0)). At least **17% of trades are wash trades, and wash-traded coins graduate more often**, so raw volume flatters fakes ([arXiv 2609.10246](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.10246)). Coins that advertised a Telegram link graduated **8.94x** more often, but only among coins that moved within about six minutes ([arXiv 2607.02823](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.02823v3)). Most number limits in the engine, such as top-10 holders under 30%, are tool habits rather than tested lines. The Auditor's follow-up of rejected coins is the right way to tune them.

### Exit rules: never sell 100% at a fixed target

The owner's "sell at X%" rule is the weakest kind of exit in every test the team has.

| Exit rule | Live 255-coin sample, lean costs | Same sample, trading-app costs | Research simulation (base case) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sell everything at +100% (2x) | −5.4% per $1 | −35.2% | −40% |
| Sell everything at +400% (5x) | −1.2% (about −3% after removing one bad price print) | −30.9% | −33% |
| Hold for 60 minutes | −9.3% | −38.5% | too noisy to call |
| Trailing stop: arm at 1.5x, sell 30% below peak, leave at 5 min if no move | not tested | not tested | +23 to +25 cents, but −7 cents without the top 0.1% of trades |

Sources: the live sample ([GeckoTerminal](https://api.geckoterminal.com/api/v2/networks/solana/new_pools?page=1)) and the team's simulation ([STRATEGY.md](../docs/STRATEGY.md)).

The live sample and the simulation agree. Tight fixed targets lose, higher targets lose less, and holding without a plan is worst, because winners give everything back. The trailing stop was the only rule with a positive average in the model, and only thanks to rare coins that run past 50x. Cap prices at 50x and it falls to about −6 cents. Remove the best 0.1% of trades and it drops to −7 cents. Real data shows the same weakness. In a public backtest of 4,293 pump.fun "callouts", removing the best 24 turned the average negative, and only 0.6% reached 10x within an hour ([Kombucho backtest](https://github.com/Kombucho/memecoin-gamble-research)). So the trailing stop is the right default, but it is not a money machine. Test 30%, 40% and 50% trails on recorded data, and sell at most 25–35% early to get your cost back. Keep the 5-minute time stop. Accept that a stop-loss will fill below its level when a coin crashes in one block.

### Stack: keep C++ and OCaml, but do not hand-build Solana transactions in them

On Solana, speed is won in three places: how early you see events, how close your server is, and how your trade lands. Each of these costs tens to hundreds of milliseconds, while the code itself runs in microseconds in any compiled language ([Dysnix](https://dysnix.com/blog/solana-arbitrage-bot-setup)). A server on the wrong continent adds about **80–120 ms** per round trip ([RPC Fast](https://rpcfast.com/blog/solana-ai-agents-for-hft)). Solana's Alpenglow upgrade went live on 21 Oct 2026 and targets about 150 ms finality, which shrinks every window further ([The Block](https://theblock.co/news/ecosystems/2026-10-21-anzas-alpenglow-goes-live-on-solana-cutting-finality-to-150-milliseconds-421508)). So C++ gives no real speed edge over Rust here, and its Solana toolkit is thin: only small community libraries such as solcpp ([solcpp](https://awesome.ecosyste.ms/projects/github.com%2Fmschneider%2Fsolcpp)). OCaml has **no Solana library at all**, though it can sign with Ed25519 ([ocaml.org](https://ocaml.org/p/mirage-crypto-ec/1.1.0/doc/Mirage_crypto_ec/Ed25519/index.html)). Jane Street's lesson is that OCaml's strict types catch bugs in the rules that guard money ([OCaml.org](https://ocaml.org/success-stories/large-scale-trading-system)), and that is exactly how the team uses it in the RiskGovernor.

The practical path to live trading keeps both languages. Let Jupiter's Swap API v2 build each transaction, and sign it in a small, separate signer process. Do not hand-write pump.fun instruction layouts in C++, where one wrong account order loses money. Run the trading loop from a server in New York, Amsterdam or Frankfurt, not from India.

### Jev: a fast scorer for text, never a trader

"JEV" is almost certainly **Jev, TypeSafe AI's "System One" decision model**. It was announced on 15 Sep 2026 and is still in waitlist early access ([DataCamp](https://www.datacamp.com/blog/system-one-models-jev)). It answers typed questions about text or JSON with a value and a probability, in a vendor-claimed **70–500 ms**. It knows nothing about markets beyond what you send it ([InfoQ](https://infoq.com/news/2026/10/typesafe-ai-jev-released)). Minor versions have already broken question types ([Real Python](https://realpython.com/ref/ai-coding-tools/jev/)). "JVI" matched nothing and is probably a typo. Jev fits the NarrativeAI slot: scoring tweets, Telegram posts and coin descriptions for hype or scam patterns, with the results cached for the trading loop to read. Pin the version, use TypeSafe's own SDK or a known gateway, and never give it keys. Live AI trading has a poor record. In Nof1's Alpha Arena, most top models lost money trading real crypto, and over-trading costs drove the results ([Decrypt](https://decrypt.co/345006/ai-crypto-trading-showdown-deepseek-grok-winning-gemini-implodes); [Protos](https://protos.com/llm-crypto-trading-contest-finds-llms-cant-trade-crypto)).

### Security: a separate burner wallet with hard limits

Bot traders mostly lose money through stolen keys, not bad trades. Banana Gun refunded about **$3M** to users drained through its bot ([Cointelegraph](https://cointelegraph.com/news/banana-gun-crypto-bot-refunds-3m)). A fake pump.fun bot on GitHub stole keys from `.env` files ([Cointelegraph](https://cointelegraph.com/news/solana-trading-bot-github-malware-scam)). In 2026, 25 fake Solana npm and PyPI packages hunted for keypairs and secrets ([JFrog](https://research.jfrog.com/post/solana-fakefix/)), and one fake repo was built to trick AI coding agents ([Hackread](https://hackread.com/fake-github-repo-ai-coding-agents-solanacry-malware/)). The safe setup starts with a separate bot wallet that holds only one day's risk ($50–$250) and is refilled from a vault. A separate signer process holds the key and enforces a daily cap and a short allowlist of programs (Jupiter, pump.fun and the token programs), which policy engines such as Turnkey's can do ([Turnkey](https://www.turnkey.com/blog/introducing-solana-policy-engine)). Keys never sit in config or environment files. The Telegram bot can only show status, pause and kill. And the FOMO wallet's exported key never goes on a server. The team must also never trade between its own members' wallets on the same coin. That is wash trading. FOMO bans it ([fomo Terms](https://fomo.family/terms)), and US prosecutors punish it: Gotbit forfeited $23M and its founder went to prison ([DOJ](https://www.justice.gov/usao-ma/pr/cryptocurrency-financial-services-firm-gotbit-and-founder-sentenced-market-manipulation)).

## India's tax turns a break-even bot into a losing one

India taxes crypto gains at a **flat 30% plus 4% cess** (plus any surcharge). Only the purchase cost can be deducted, and **losses cannot offset any gain or be carried forward** ([Income Tax Department](https://www.incometaxindia.gov.in/documents/20117/42998/Taxation-of-Virtual-Digital-Asset-VDA_2026-01-06_06-43-57_cfe04a_en.pdf/153706c8-7b5c-77d4-fc56-624c50ece1b0)). Budget 2026 left this unchanged ([Decrypt](https://decrypt.co/356601)). A **1% TDS** still applies to transfers. The old section 194S became section 393(1) of the Income-tax Act, 2025, from 1 Apr 2026 ([TDSMAN](https://blog.tdsman.com/2026/05/tds-on-virtual-digital-assets-section-3931-194s/)). On a DEX there is no counterparty to deduct it, and no official rule settles who should ([KoinX](https://www.koinx.com/in/tax-guides/tds-on-crypto-trades-on-foreign-exchanges-and-dexs/)). Every swap is a taxable transfer: SOL into a coin is one, and the coin back into SOL is another. Each is valued in rupees at that moment.

For a bot that trades a lot, the math is brutal. Say the winning trades gain ₹10 lakh and the losing trades lose ₹9 lakh. The bot made ₹1 lakh. But the tax is 30% of the full ₹10 lakh plus cess, about ₹3.12 lakh, which leaves the team **about ₹2.1 lakh down**. A bot that only breaks even before tax loses about 31% of its gross gains once tax is paid. Fees and tips are probably not deductible either, so confirm that with a chartered accountant. India also keeps blocking offshore platforms that skip FIU-IND registration, most recently 15 platforms in Sep 2026 ([YourStory](https://yourstory.com/2026/09/india-orders-takedown-15-offshore-crypto-platforms-aml-non-compliance)). Whether FOMO is registered is unknown. Keep a per-swap rupee ledger from day one (Agent 20 can do it), and move money in and out through an FIU-registered Indian exchange.

## Conclusion

The deepest problem is that the plan's two goals fight each other. The checks that keep a bot safe are: wait three minutes, and demand real liquidity, real buyers and a sellable quote. Those same checks put it after the move. Coins that make the big run from launch mostly do it in the first two minutes, and creators holding near-free supply sell to whoever arrives next. More agents cannot close that gap; only a different target can. The evidence leaves two honest options. One is to copy the FOMO winners' style: few coins, entered later at $400K–$3M, sized to matter, held for hours or days and sold in steps. In that role the engine works as a screener and safety checker, not a machine that fires $1 at everything. The other is to keep the engine as a lab and accept that it may prove there is no edge to find.

The paper run was worth far more than the $0.93 it lost. In 45 minutes it found a real sell price 37% below the screen price and a "hard" filter that leaked. Both would have cost real money in live trading. That is the engine's true value: a cheap way to learn the truth before money is at risk. The next win is to make that truth hard to argue with: write down the go/no-go test now, run the replay, and let the numbers decide.

## Update after the report: both engine flaws fixed

Both flaws found in the paper run were fixed and tested the same day (branch `claude/tender-cerf-sdzlws`):

- **Exits now use the real sell price.** Every open position is marked at a real Jupiter sell quote (up to 3 quotes per 4-second monitor pass; between quotes the mark moves with the screen price times the last quote/screen ratio). Peaks track sellable value, and no exit fires on a mark older than 15 seconds until a fresh quote confirms it. See `engine/src/app/orchestrator.cpp` and `Executor::mark` in `engine/src/agents/trading.cpp`.
- **Hard rejects are permanent.** A hard-rejected coin goes on a ban list that outlives the 400-coin watch list, so a later boost or profile lead cannot bring it back.
- The time stop now matches the simulated rule: leave after 5 minutes if the coin is below 1.3x.
- New tests cover both fixes (engine tests: 126 passing; OCaml risk tests: 18 passing).
